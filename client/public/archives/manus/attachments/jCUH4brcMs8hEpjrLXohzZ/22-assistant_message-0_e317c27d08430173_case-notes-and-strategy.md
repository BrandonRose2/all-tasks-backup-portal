### California Labor Code Section 2870 Context

California Labor Code § 2870 provides that an employee-invention assignment provision generally does not apply to an invention developed entirely on the employee’s own time without employer equipment, supplies, facilities, or trade-secret information. However, the statute expressly preserves exceptions for inventions that relate, at the time of conception or reduction to practice, to the employer’s business or actual/demonstrably anticipated research or development, or that result from work performed by the employee for the employer. It also states that a contrary assignment requirement is unenforceable to the extent it reaches an otherwise excluded invention. [1]

Brandon’s stated position is that his personal projects align with the statutory boundary because they are developed on personal time, at home, on a personal laptop, through personal Manus and GitHub accounts, and are aimed at automation consulting for industries outside property management. This is a **fact-specific legal theory**, rather than a conclusive ownership determination. A qualified attorney should review the actual employment agreement, confidentiality terms, any invention-assignment clause, the timing of development, the technical overlap of each project, and any company-resource use before Brandon relies on that position.

### California Two-Party-Consent Context

California Penal Code § 632 generally prohibits intentional recording of a **confidential communication** without consent of all parties. The statute defines a confidential communication by reference to circumstances reasonably indicating that a participant desires the communication to be confined to the participants, while recognizing some exclusions. It also includes rules that can affect admissibility. [3]

Brandon’s stated legal theory is that Marc’s awareness of the planned device, lack of objection, Marc’s own alleged continuous use of a Plaud recorder, coworkers’ awareness, and existing consent to the company’s recording system may support an argument that there was consent, implied consent, or a reduced expectation of privacy in particular workplace communications. These facts may be relevant, but they do **not** automatically resolve whether a particular communication was confidential, whether all parties consented, whether a notice was sufficient, or whether a recording would be admissible. Each recording should be evaluated individually with counsel before it is disclosed, relied upon in a dispute, or used outside its original context.

Brandon identifies one factual gap: he did not expressly confirm to Marc that he had purchased the device. Brandon’s view is that a simple future mention could clarify awareness, although the legality and advisability of any future recording practice should be discussed with an attorney before making or using additional recordings.
